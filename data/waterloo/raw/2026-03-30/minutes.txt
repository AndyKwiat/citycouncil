1	1. TERRITORIAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT
1	Mayor McCabe opened the meeting with the following Territorial Acknowledgement:
1	We would like to begin by acknowledging that the land on which we gather (land on
1	which we are broadcasting from) today is the land traditionally cared for by the
1	Haudenosaunee, Anishinaabe and Chonnontan People. We also acknowledge the
1	enduring presence and deep traditional knowledge and philosophies of the Indigenous
1	People with whom we share this land today.
1	Tomorrow, March 31st, is National Indigenous Languages Day.
1	There are over 60 unique Indigenous language dialects spoken in Canada,
1	and all are considered to be critically endangered, except the dialects of Inuktitut. The
1	number of Indigenous language speakers continues to decline. Contact between
1	Indigenous and European languages has led to the borrowing of many Indigenous
1	words into English and French as well as the creation of new language varieties.
1	There has been a continued dedication to the reclamation and revitalization of
1	Indigenous languages.
1	With respect to my personal and professional reconciliation journey, I grew up on a farm
1	outside of Kenilworth, Ontario. The land was also the traditional territory of the
1	Anishinaabe and Haudenosaunee people. My grandparents owned this farm and my
1	parents purchased it when they got married.
2	The area was covered by multiple treaties, most notably Treaty 45.5 signed in 1836,
2	also known as the Saugeen Tract Purchase.
2	Historically, this area was utilized by the Attawandaron people however, according to
2	research, the confederacy was dispersed in the mid 17th century by disease and war
2	with the Haudenosaunee – around 1653. The land was heavily surveyed and described
2	as “empty wilderness waiting for European settlement.” By 1836, the culture of the
2	Attawandaron and the Haudenosaunee people was considered destroyed.
2	As Mayor, I continue my learning and reconciliation journey.
2	I’ve walked in UW’s Truth and Reconciliation Walks, helped paint a bit of Laurier’s
2	beautiful First Nations mural, attended local Pow Wows and City of Waterloo Museum
2	exhibits, and participated in training opportunities about Sacred Fires. I’ve read local
2	authors, such as North Wind Man, and other authors, notably Tanya Talaga. Seven
2	Fallen Feathers particularly has stayed with me. I’ve toured the Mohawk Institute
2	Residential School aka the MUSH Hole, in Brantford, that operated until June 27, 1970
2	as Canada’s longest running residential school, running for 142 years.
2	Now it is the Woodland Cultural Centre, and again, I encourage everyone to make time
2	to go to this important centre. I’m grateful for the advice and work of Indigenous Advisor
2	Brian Hill, his work at the City, and with the Association of Municipalities of Ontario on
2	AMO’s first Indigenous Action Plan.
2	Over my 3 years, I’ve met with Six Nations of the Grand River Chief Hill, Mississauga’s
2	of the Credit former Chief Sault as well as local First Nations’ leaders:
2	Donna Dubie, Healing of the Seven Generations; Elder Mygeeun Henry, UW
2	Indigenous Advisor; Dr. Darin Johnston, Laurier VP Indigenous Affairs; Professor
2	Clarence Cachagee, Crowshield Lodge; Bangishimo and Amy Smoke, Land Back;
2	White Owl Mike Duke; Anastasia Lyons, Firekeeper; Laury Turcotte, KW Urban Native
2	Wigwam; Tina Rowe, Southwestern Ontario Aboriginal Health Access Centre; Renata
2	Richards, Healing of the Seven Generations and others.
2	I’m grateful that they offered the opportunity to continue to build our relationship and to
2	meet each season. Very soon, I will again meet with Chief Hill as she continues to meet
2	with Mayors in each of the 47 municipalities in the Haldimand Tract.
2	I’m grateful for the time, the patience and the learning that every First Nations’ leader
2	and resident has provided me.
2	Of course, there is more to do. And I remain committed to learning.

3	Mayor McCabe added an additional moment of reflection for Transgender Day of
3	Visibility:
3	Today is the International Transgender Day of Visibility. This annual awareness day
3	honors the lives of transgender people while shedding light on the discrimination and
3	challenges they continue to face. Visibility plays a crucial role in challenging the stigma
3	and misconceptions surrounding transgender people. It also emphasizes the diversity
3	within the transgender community, reminding us that transgender individuals are not a
3	monolithic group but rather a vibrant community with many different experiences,
3	identities and needs. As a community, we’re committed to fostering safe spaces for self-
3	discovery and taking action on oppressive systems that perpetrate violence and harm.
