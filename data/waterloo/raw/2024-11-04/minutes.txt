2	3. TERRITORIAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT
2	Councillor Wright opened the meeting with the following Territorial Acknowledgement:
2	We would like to begin by acknowledging that the land on which many of us
2	are gathered today is the land traditionally cared for by the
2	Haudenosaunee, Anishnaabe and Chonnonton peoples. We also
2	acknowledge the enduring presence and deep traditional knowledge and
2	philosophies of the Indigenous people with whom we share this land today.
2	Councillor Wright offered further thoughts on watershed governance and the
2	recommendations of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. She offered reflections
2	on the passing of The Honourable Murray Sinclair, and on Treaties Recognition Week.
2	Treaties Recognition Week takes place annually, from November 3 to 9.
2	This week is an opportunity to reflect on the significance of Treaties in
2	local history and ongoing relationships with Indigenous communities.
2	The City of Waterloo commissioned three replica Wampum belts: The
2	Hiawatha, The Two-Row and The Dish with One Spoon. In June,
2	Kahlunyunti (Martin Powless) of Oneida Nation of the Thames, the artist
2	who recreated the belts, presented the belts to Council and provided some
2	teaching on their history.
2	These belts are a physical representation of the oral history of treaties and
2	settlement of the area around the Great Lakes Region extending along a
2	portion of the St. Lawrence River, which we call home. These are
2	significant representations of our colonial history and are still seen today
2	as a reminder to all of us of the responsibilities we hold as treaty people.
3	The Wampum belts are currently on display in the lobby of Waterloo City
3	Hall.
3	As we continue to reflect on Treaty rights and relationships, we must
3	commit to honouring these agreements and understanding their impact on
3	the lives of Indigenous peoples today. This is essential for Reconciliation
3	and ensuring that Indigenous voices are recognized and respected in
3	discussions that affect their rights and territories.
3	Together, we can build a future grounded in respect and collaboration
3	between Indigenous and non-Indigenous communities.
3	The Hiawatha Wampum (year 1100 approx.): Depicts and describes the
3	formation of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy under a pledge of peace
3	and understanding between the five nations comprising the Seneca,
3	Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida and Mohawk tribes.
3	The Two-Row Wampum (year 1613 approx.): Describes the relationship
3	between the incoming colonizing peoples and Indigenous peoples.
3	Specifically, between the Mohawks and the Dutch colonizers, moving
3	forward together in parallel, but never merging, signifying respect for each
3	other’s rights.
3	The Dish with One Spoon Wampum (year 1701 but could be the same age
3	as Hiawatha Wampum): Describes an agreement for sharing hunting
3	territory among two or more First Nation. There is a recognition that all
3	Peoples are eating out of a single dish, that is, all hunting in the shared
3	territory. One spoon signifies that all Peoples sharing the territory are
3	expected to limit the game they take to leave enough for others and for the
3	continued abundance and viability of the hunting grounds into the future.
